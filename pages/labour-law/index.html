What most foreign employers don't realize about hiring in Spain

Hiring in Spain isn't complicated because the law is unclear. It's complicated because the rules changed significantly in 2021, and a lot of advice online still reflects how things worked before that.

01

Assuming a temporary contract is the safer, more flexible option: since the 2021 reform, permanent contracts are the default, and a temporary one without a genuinely valid reason gets reclassified, with real cost attached.

02

Not registering an employee with Social Security before their first day: there's no grace period; it has to be done in advance, not once payroll is due.

03

Underestimating the real cost of an employee: employer social security contributions typically add somewhere around 30% on top of gross salary, not a token amount.

04

Treating a dismissal as a simple decision: one without solid cause or the correct procedure gets reclassified as unfair, and the compensation owed increases significantly.

05

Not checking the collective agreement that applies to your business: it can set higher minimum salaries and different conditions than the general law, and it applies whether you knew about it or not.

None of this needs to catch you out when someone who does this regularly is checking it before you sign a contract, not after.

Frequently asked questions about labour law in Spain

In most cases, yes. Since the 2021 labour reform, permanent contracts are the default in practice, and temporary contracts are only valid for narrow, specific reasons, such as covering a genuine and temporary spike in demand or replacing an employee who's absent. Using a temporary contract outside those cases gets it reclassified as permanent, with backdated obligations attached.

Employer social security contributions typically add somewhere around 30% on top of gross salary, covering common contingencies, unemployment, workplace accident insurance, and a few smaller items. The exact figure depends on your sector's accident-risk rate, so we calculate it for your specific case rather than quoting a number that doesn't apply to you.

A labour inspection or the employee themselves can challenge it, and if a temporary contract doesn't meet the legal requirements, it's treated as if it had been permanent from the start. That carries back pay, social security, and dismissal implications you weren't planning for.

Yes. Registration with Social Security has to happen before the employee starts work, not on the same day after the fact and not within some grace period. There isn't one.

A dismissal with a solid, documented cause and the correct procedure is generally fair, and the compensation owed is lower. A dismissal that a court finds unjustified, or that skipped the correct procedure, is ruled unfair and carries a significantly higher compensation, currently 33 days of salary per year worked, capped at 24 months' pay.
