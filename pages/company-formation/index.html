What most foreign founders don't realize about setting up a company in Spain

Incorporating a Spanish company isn't hard because the rules are complicated. It's hard because the steps depend on each other in ways that aren't obvious until one of them stalls.

01

Assuming any company name works: the Companies Registry can reject it, costing days you didn't plan for.

02

Not knowing the bank needs the reserved name certificate before it will even open an account for a company that doesn't legally exist yet.

03

Treating the role of director as a formality, when Spanish administrators carry personal liability — civil, tax, and in some cases even criminal — not just a title.

04

Forgetting the foreign investment declaration that non-resident shareholders are legally required to file once the company is set up.

05

Discovering after incorporation that dividends sent abroad are withheld at source, unless a tax treaty says otherwise.

None of this needs to slow you down when someone who does this regularly is handling the sequence for you.

How the process works: step by step

01

Initial consultation

We ask about your business, how many shareholders are involved, and how much capital you're starting with, because that determines the right structure from the outset.

02

Reserving the company name

We request the certificate from the Companies Registry confirming your chosen name is available, and have a backup ready in case it isn't.

03

Opening the bank account and depositing capital

We coordinate the account opening and the capital deposit, and obtain the bank certificate the notary will need to see.

04

Drafting the bylaws and signing the incorporation deed

We draft the bylaws around your business, then sign the deed with you at the notary — in person, or on your behalf by power of attorney if you can't travel.

05

Registering the company

We apply for the company's tax number, file the foreign investment declaration if it applies, and register the company at the Companies Registry.

06

Getting you trading

We register the company for VAT and Social Security, and put its first filing deadlines on the calendar, so nothing is missed in the first quarter.

No. Non-residents can be shareholders and directors of a Spanish company without living in the country. The one requirement is an NIE for every shareholder and director, and if you can't attend the signing in person, a power of attorney lets someone else sign on your behalf.

A limited company (SL) separates your personal assets from the business — your liability is generally limited to what you've invested. As a self-employed worker (autónomo), there's no such separation, and your personal assets are exposed to the business's debts. An SL also tends to look more established to banks, suppliers, and larger clients.

The traditional minimum for an SL is €3,000, though current law allows incorporation with as little as €1. Starting below €3,000 comes with conditions: the company must set aside part of its annual profit into a legal reserve until it reaches €3,000, and shareholders can be personally liable for the shortfall if the company is wound up while owing money. Starting closer to €3,000 avoids that complication.

Yes. A single-shareholder limited company is entirely legal in Spain, and it's a common structure for foreign entrepreneurs setting up alone.

Quarterly VAT returns, quarterly withholding filings if applicable, an annual corporate tax return, and annual accounts filed at the Companies Registry. Newly incorporated companies are generally taxed at a reduced 15% rate on the first two profitable tax years, rather than the standard rate. There's also a technical requirement coming into force for invoicing software, known as Verifactu, with a deadline of 1 January 2027 for companies, so it's worth planning for it early rather than leaving it to the last minute.

Generally yes, Spain withholds tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders, though the rate can be reduced if there's a double taxation treaty between Spain and your country of residence. We check whether one applies before assuming the standard rate.
